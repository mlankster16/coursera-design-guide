Accessibility, Copyright & AI

As you develop each Learning Asset, you’ll make choices that help it work well for learners, fit a public and commercial Coursera Course, and reflect your own expertise (even if AI has supported the process).

Build in accessibility

Accessibility is part of every Duke Coursera Learning Asset and is easiest to support when considered from the beginning. Share ideas and drafts with your Learning Experience Designer while there is still time to shape the approach together.

Text and structure

  • Use a logical heading structure and consistent formatting.
  • Explain specialized terminology or syntax.
  • Write link text that makes sense on its own.

Images and visuals

  • Provide alternative text for meaningful images.
  • Describe charts, diagrams, and data in the surrounding content.
  • Check color contrast, including on slides.

Video and audio

  • Leave the lower third of slides empty for captions.
  • Describe meaningful visual information aloud.
  • Record with clear audio.

Interaction and navigation

  • Where possible, make essential controls reachable by keyboard and assistive technology.
  • When you can, choose interactions that don’t depend on dragging, hovering, or precise movement.
  • Offer an accessible static version of interactive content.

Multiple ways to engage

  • Pair color, sound, or position with another way to understand the information.
  • Give instructions and feedback learners can return to.
  • Share documents in accessible formats; your Learning Experience Designer can help.

Alternative paths

  • Offer another way to participate when a format creates a barrier.
  • Consider the accessibility of third-party tools, editors, and consoles too.
  • Ask learners only for information the activity needs.

Share with your Learning Experience Designer

  • Editable source files whenever possible
  • What key images, charts, or media are meant to communicate
  • Any tool, plugin, software, or file format learners will use
  • Questions about alternatives or possible barriers

Copyright and permissions

Coursera is a public, global, commercial platform. Materials you already use in teaching may need additional review, permission, or licensing. Whenever possible, build from materials you created yourself.

In a Duke classroom

Shared with a defined group of enrolled students. Classroom exceptions, fair use, library licenses, or Course Reserves may support limited use.

In a Coursera Course

Distributed publicly and commercially, often for years. Materials generally need to be created for the Course, in the public domain, licensed for commercial use, or used with permission.

Take note of anything you didn’t create

Text and excerpts, images and figures, video and music, datasets, code and software, real people or proprietary cases, and external tools.

Your CTL team can help you decide what may be used, what needs permission, and what to replace. Linking to a lawful source often works better than posting.

Fair use is decided case by case

A commercial context may weigh against it. If you’re hoping to rely on fair use, talk it through with your CTL team.

Materials like political cartoons, Getty images, popular films, songs, and trademarks generally need permission before they can be reprinted.

Creative Commons licenses you can use on Coursera
LicenseWhat to keep in mind
CC0The creator has waived copyright restrictions to the extent legally possible. Attribution is appreciated even when it is not required.
CC BYCommercial use and adaptation are allowed. Credit the creator and provide the license information.

It’s worth checking the exact license, not just the Creative Commons symbol. Other CC licenses usually aren’t compatible with Coursera, and your CTL team can help you check.

Share with your CTL team

  • A link to the original source
  • The creator or rights holder, when known
  • Any license, permission, or purchase information
  • How you’d like to use it: reproduce, adapt, embed, or link

Using AI during Course development

AI tools can be useful for brainstorming, drafting, restructuring, summarizing, or generating examples and practice questions. They can also introduce errors, bias, privacy concerns, and copyright questions. Your expertise remains central.

CTL will not use AI to develop course materials with your content without express permission.

Protect what goes in

  • Share only material you have permission to share.
  • Leave out confidential, proprietary, and personal information.
  • Consider what the provider may store or reuse.

Review what comes out

  • Verify facts, figures, citations, and reasoning.
  • Check for bias, close reproduction, and accessibility.
  • Revise to reflect your expertise and voice.

Keep your CTL team informed

  • Let us know when AI contributed substantially to an asset.
  • Share the tool and how you reviewed its output.
  • Bring any open questions about accuracy or rights.

Before you hand off an asset

Talk through these with your Learning Experience Designer. Decisions and source links can be recorded in the Design Document.
