Designing a Coursera Course

A well-designed Course brings together knowledge and skills that learners can apply in a meaningful context. Define what learners should be able to do by the end of the Course, then use those Course objectives to plan the support learners will need and organize the Modules, activities, feedback, and assessments that help them succeed.

1 Step 1 of 5

Write Course objectives

Once the Course has a clear purpose, translate that purpose into 2–4 Course-level learning objectives. Use the table below to move from the subject of the Course to what learners will be able to demonstrate. A Course-level objective typically brings together relevant knowledge and skills in a meaningful context.

Parts of a Course objective, what each part identifies, and an example
Element What it helps identify Example
Topic The subject or area the Course addresses Business data
Knowledge What learners should know or understand about the topic Characteristics of useful business data
Skill Something learners can practice and demonstrate Evaluate a dataset using defined quality criteria
Course-level objective What learners will accomplish by applying their knowledge and skills in a meaningful context Determine whether available data is relevant, complete, and suitable for addressing a business question

Draft your objectives

Use the following stem to begin each objective:

By the end of this Course, learners will be able to…

Consider using observable action verbs such as

analyze apply create compare design evaluate identify interpret recommend communicate

Some verbs may need more specificity

understand learn know become familiar with explore

These verbs may describe your instructional intention, but on their own, they do not clearly indicate what learners will be able to demonstrate. 

Complete the verb with what learners will act on and, when helpful, the context or criteria. A broad instructional intention such as “understand how data can support a business decision” can be translated into different observable objectives, depending on what learners should demonstrate:

  • ·Evaluate a dataset for relevance and completeness.
  • ·Recommend a data source for a specific business question.
  • ·Design a simple analysis plan to address a business need.

Choose the objective(s) that best match what learners should be able to demonstrate by the end of the Course.

Worked example

Writing Course objectives for Foundations of Data Analytics for Business

Show example

Throughout this guide, we use Data Analytics for Business Decisions as a worked example. On the Specialization page, you saw how its three Courses work together. Here, we’ll look more closely at the first Course, Foundations of Data Analytics for Business, and translate its purpose into Course-level objectives.

Specialization: Data Analytics for Business Decisions

Course 1: Foundations of Data Analytics for Business

Learners begin by framing a business question and considering what data they would need to investigate it. They then identify and evaluate available data before preparing a simple dataset for analysis.

Learners leave with: a focused business question and a simple, analysis-ready dataset.

That purpose can be translated into a small set of observable Course-level objectives.

By the end of the Course, learners will be able to:

  • Translate a business need into a focused question that can be investigated with data.
  • Identify the data needed to investigate a business question.
  • Evaluate a dataset for relevance, completeness, and basic quality concerns.
  • Prepare a simple dataset for an introductory business analysis.

These objectives describe what learners can demonstrate, rather than topics the instructor intends to cover. For example, “data quality” names a subject. “Evaluate a dataset for relevance, completeness, and common quality issues” describes what learners will do with their knowledge of that subject.

2 Step 2 of 5

Plan what each objective will require

Course objectives are works in progress at this stage. As you explore what learners will need, you may discover that an objective needs to be narrowed, expanded, or reworded.

For each objective, talk through four questions with your Learning Experience Designer:

  1. What will learners need to know, notice, decide, or do?
  2. What content, examples, or expertise do you already have?
  3. What might need to be developed, updated, or sourced?
  4. What questions do you have about learners, workforce relevance, or what is possible in Coursera?
3 Step 3 of 5

Group related learning into Modules

Once you have explored what each objective will require, look across your notes for ideas that belong together.

Ask:

  • ·Which ideas, skills, or decisions could learners develop together?
  • ·Which goals might be supported by the same examples or resources?
  • ·What could learners practice or demonstrate together?
  • ·Could these related goals form a coherent Module?

A Module may support more than one Course objective, and one objective may be developed across several Modules. Working titles or broad themes are appropriate at this stage.

Complete your Course structure

Use your planning notes to draft working Module titles and summarize what learners will be able to do after completing each Module.

The Module order may reflect the structure of your existing Course or program. It does not need to mirror the order in which every concept is first introduced. Working titles and outcomes can continue to change as you develop each Module.

4 Step 4 of 5

Design for flexibility

Coursera Courses often work well when they are modular and self-contained. When possible, provide enough context for learners to understand the Course's purpose, why the learning matters, and what prior knowledge or skills they need to succeed without requiring them to have completed a specific earlier Course.

Some subjects are best learned through a sequence. When learners genuinely need knowledge or skills from an earlier Course, make that dependency clear. The goal is not to eliminate prerequisites, but to make Courses as portable and understandable on their own as the learning allows.

More modular

This Course assumes familiarity with basic spreadsheet formulas and functions.

More dependent on a specific sequence

As you learned in Course 1, you will now build on the framework introduced in Module 3…

Ask:

  • ·What context do learners need to understand the purpose of this Course?
  • ·What prior knowledge or skills should we name?
  • ·Can learners complete the Course without relying on a previous Course?
  • ·If a sequence is necessary, have we made that dependency clear?

Foundations of Data Analytics for Business

Course description

Best practice

Course description

✕

Describe the Course from the learner's perspective. Lead with what learners will gain or be able to do, rather than with a list of topics. Provide enough context for the Course to make sense on its own, even when it is part of a larger learning pathway.

Designed for professionals, students, and career changers who want to use data more effectively in business settings, this beginner-level course introduces the process of turning a business need into a question that data can help answer. Learners will practice framing business questions, identifying data requirements, evaluating data for relevance and quality, and preparing a simple dataset for analysis.

Through realistic business examples, guided practice, quizzes, and applied activities, learners will build foundational skills in business problem framing, data requirements analysis, data quality assessment, and spreadsheet-based data preparation. These skills can help learners communicate more effectively with data professionals, make more informed decisions, and prepare for further study or roles involving business analytics.

Taught by Jordan Lee, a faculty member specializing in business analytics and evidence-informed decision-making, the course offers an applied, self-paced introduction to the full process from business question to analysis-ready dataset.
