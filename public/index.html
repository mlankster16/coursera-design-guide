What is Coursera?

Coursera learners come from around the world and bring different backgrounds, goals, and levels of experience. Many are working professionals studying on their own schedule, without an instructor present to answer questions in the moment.

Self-paced and applied

Courses are shorter than a semester and organized around skills learners can use.

Designed for a broad audience

Learners may be professionals, students, career changers, or people exploring a new subject.

Support is built into the materials

Explanation, practice, feedback, and assessment need to be part of the experience itself.

Public and global

Materials may reach learners beyond Duke and remain available beyond a single academic term.

Note on terminology

In this guide, terms like Specialization, Course, Module, Learning Assets, Readings, etc. refer to Coursera learning products. They are capitalized intentionally to distinguish the Coursera learning experience from a traditional for-credit course, class session, or academic program. 

How the pieces fit together

A Coursera learning experience is built from connected pieces: a Specialization includes Courses, a Course includes Modules, and a Module includes the Learning Assets learners use along the way. Some Modules organize those assets into optional Lessons.

Review the materials you already have

Most existing materials can be adapted. You and your Learning Experience Designer will look at six things together as you decide what can be adapted for a Coursera audience.

Start with skills

Begin with what learners should be able to do.

+

Coursera learners are often professionals building a specific skill. We’ll begin with what they should be able to do by the end, then work backward to the materials, framing, and assessments that will help them get there.

Keep videos focused

Shorter, focused videos tend to work best for learners.

+

Learners tend to engage best with videos under about ten minutes. We can help identify natural breaks in longer recordings and shape them into shorter, focused segments.

Balance learning formats

Readings can be valuable, but learners also need opportunities to practice.

+

A course made up mostly of readings may not provide enough variety or practice. Together, we’ll look at where a video, audio conversation, visual, or hands-on activity might communicate an idea more effectively.

Build in accessibility

It is easier to plan for accessibility before materials are produced.

+

Clear structure, readable language, accessible documents and slides, meaningful alt text, appropriate color contrast, and multiple ways to engage with content help make a course more usable and welcoming for everyone.

Check content permissions

Materials created by others may need clearance for public, commercial use.

+

Images, figures, video clips, datasets, and other materials you did not create may require permission or an appropriate license. We’ll review them together to make sure they can be used with a public, global audience.

Plan for tool access

Choose tools learners can access freely and widely.

+

Paid or license-restricted tools may be out of reach for learners outside Duke or unavailable in some countries. When a tool is essential, we’ll plan together for how learners can access it.
